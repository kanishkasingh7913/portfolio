Business Context

A growth ceiling built into the process

HeyCoach sold paid DSA and System Design courses to software developers — primarily engineers at service companies like TCS, Infosys, and Wipro who wanted to break into product-based companies like Google, Amazon, and Flipkart. High intent, clear motivation, busy professionals.

The product was good. Marketing was working. Leads were coming in. But every single conversion required a human sales rep to get on a 45–60 minute call. The entire sales motion was manual, templated, and had no way to scale without hiring more people.

The sales team was talented. But they were spending 80% of their time running the same 45-minute script on every lead — regardless of how ready that lead actually was to pay.

Problem

Three problems, one broken process

The manual sales call created a cascade of problems that compounded each other. Fixing one without fixing the others wouldn't move the needle.

Scheduling delays killed high-intent momentum A developer who was ready to buy on Tuesday had to wait until Thursday for a call. By then, they'd either gone cold or signed up with a competitor. The delay between intent and action was the single biggest conversion killer.
Drop-off during the wait was significant Leads who filled the form and then waited for a call had a high chance of not showing up. Every handoff in the funnel — from form to call scheduling, from call to offer letter — was a potential exit point.
Sales team was running a repeatable script, not having conversations After analysing multiple sales calls, a clear pattern emerged: 80% of the call followed the same decision tree. The same questions, the same qualification logic, the same offer generation. This wasn't relationship selling — it was a workflow that could be productised.

The key insight that unlocked the solution

The sales call wasn't adding unique human value for most leads — it was executing a repeatable, templated decision tree. If we could replicate that logic in a product flow, the majority of users didn't need a human at all. The ones who did would get a better experience because the sales team would have time to actually focus on them.

Solution

A progressive self-serve journey

The solution wasn't to remove humans entirely — it was to remove humans from the path of users who didn't need them. The new flow was designed to be progressive: fast-track high-intent users to payment, and give uncertain users enough context to make their own decision — or escalate to a sales rep if they genuinely needed it.

Progressive screening — not a gate, a path

The questionnaire was designed to feel like a personalised experience, not a form. Each question captured intent signals — experience level, goal timeline, budget readiness — and used that data to generate a tailored offer letter instantly. Users who showed high-intent markers could skip optional steps entirely with a fast-track path.

Automated offer letter generation

The offer letter was previously generated manually by the sales team after the call — another handoff, another delay. The new system generated it in real time at the end of the questionnaire, personalised to the user's stated profile. No human needed to review or send it.

End-to-end self-serve payment

Token payment — previously only possible after a call and a manually sent payment link — was integrated directly into the flow. A user could go from form submission to paid enrolment in a single session without any human interaction.

The Hardest Part

The product worked. Getting people to use it didn't.

Shipping the technical flow was straightforward. The real challenge was internal adoption. The sales team had built their entire workflow — and in some ways their sense of professional value — around the screening call. Replacing that with an automated system felt threatening, not helpful.

The risk: A product nobody internally trusts will never reach users. If the sales team routed leads around the new flow, kept scheduling calls, or undermined the system in any way, it would fail regardless of how good the product was. Sales team trust wasn't a nice-to-have. It was a launch requirement.

How I approached it

Weekly sync meetings throughout the build Brought the sales team into the process early and kept them there. Every decision that touched their workflow was discussed with them, not presented to them.
Actively collected and acted on their feedback Every piece of feedback the sales team gave was triaged seriously. If they said the questionnaire was missing a question they always asked — we added it. If they felt the offer generation logic was wrong for edge cases — we fixed it. Iteration speed built credibility.
Framed it as freeing them, not replacing them The narrative shifted from "the product is doing your job" to "the product handles the templated cases so you can focus on the complex, high-value ones." The 20% of leads who genuinely needed a nuanced conversation would now get a better version of the sales team — not a distracted one running their 50th screening call of the week.

The biggest PM learning from this project: product adoption is a product problem. If internal stakeholders don't trust the system, the most technically sound solution fails at the last mile. Designing for adoption — not just for users — is a core PM responsibility.

Key Product Decisions

The decisions that shaped the flow

1
Progressive screening over a single long form

The questionnaire could have been one 10-question form. Instead it was progressive — each step built on the previous answer, showed a progress indicator, and only asked what was relevant to that user's stated profile. This reduced perceived friction significantly. A user who indicated high readiness in step 2 was shown fewer steps overall. A user who indicated uncertainty was shown additional context and reassurance. The same information was collected — just in an experience that felt personalised rather than bureaucratic.

2
Keep the sales call as an option, not remove it entirely

The temptation was to remove the sales call completely and force everyone through the self-serve path. I pushed back on this. A segment of users — particularly those making a significant financial decision for the first time, or those with specific situations the questionnaire couldn't fully capture — genuinely needed a human conversation. Removing that option would have hurt conversion for that segment and undermined the sales team's trust in the new system. The call remained available as an opt-in for users who wanted it, surfaced at a specific point in the flow.

3
Real-time offer generation, not a delayed email

The original proposal was to generate the offer letter and email it within a few hours. I argued against this. The whole point of the self-serve flow was to remove the wait. Sending an email and asking the user to come back later reintroduced the exact drop-off risk we were solving for. The offer letter had to appear in the same session, on the same screen, immediately after the questionnaire. This required tighter integration with the offer generation logic but the conversion impact justified it.

4
Token payment first, full payment later

Full course payment upfront created too much friction for a first interaction. Token payment — a small amount to confirm intent and secure the enrolment — lowered the commitment threshold significantly. Users who paid the token were then nurtured toward full payment through a separate follow-up flow. This was a deliberate funnel decision: optimise the self-serve flow for activation (token), not for immediate revenue (full payment).

The 95% completion rate was the number that mattered most to me. It meant the flow wasn't just technically functional — users were actually finishing it. A drop-off rate of only 5% across the entire automated journey validated that the progressive design, the fast-track path, and the in-session offer generation were working together as intended.

The 80% reduction in manual calls meant the sales team could redirect their time toward genuinely complex cases — users with edge-case situations, high-value leads who wanted a relationship, and post-enrolment support. The quality of those conversations improved because the volume of routine calls dropped.

₹25L+ in incremental revenue is the business result. But the metric I'd track most carefully in a v2 would be time-from-form-to-payment. That number — measured in days before, and minutes after — is the clearest proof that the bottleneck was removed.

Reflection

What this project taught me

Internal adoption is a product problem.

The biggest lesson from this project wasn't about product design — it was about change management as a product discipline. A technically perfect solution that internal stakeholders don't trust will fail at the last mile. Designing for adoption — building trust through iteration speed, genuine incorporation of feedback, and reframing the narrative — is as much a PM skill as writing a PRD.

Replicate the logic, not the format.

The temptation when replacing a human process with a product is to recreate the human interaction digitally — a chatbot that asks the same questions a sales rep would ask, in the same order, in a conversational UI. The better approach is to understand the underlying decision logic and design a flow that achieves the same outcome in a format native to the product. Progressive screening isn't a sales call in a browser — it's a fundamentally different interaction that achieves the same goal more efficiently.

What I'd do differently.

I'd instrument time-to-payment from day one and run it as a live dashboard visible to both the product team and the sales team. Making the metric shared — and letting the sales team see in real time how the automated flow was performing — would have accelerated their trust in the system instead of building it gradually over weeks of syncs.
